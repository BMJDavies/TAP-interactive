Example 7.1.2 The square root of 2.

Prove that \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.
🔗
Solution.
Assume, for contradiction, that \(\sqrt{2}\) is rational. Then \(\sqrt{2} = \dfrac{p}{q}\) for some integers \(p\) and \(q\) with \(q \neq 0\text{,}\) and we may choose the fraction \(\dfrac{p}{q}\) in its simplest form. Squaring,
\begin{gather*} \sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad p^2 = 2q^2. \end{gather*}
So \(p^2\) is even, and therefore \(p\) is even too (we prove this fact in Example 7.4.1). As \(p\) is even, \(p = 2k\) for some integer \(k\text{,}\) and so \(p^2 = (2k)^2 = 4k^2\text{.}\) From \(p^2 = 2q^2\) and \(p^2 = 4k^2\) we get \(2q^2 = 4k^2\text{,}\) which gives \(q^2 = 2k^2\text{.}\) In other words, \(q^2\) is even, and therefore \(q\) is even too.
🔗
But if \(p\) and \(q\) are both even, then \(\dfrac{p}{q}\) is not in its simplest form, which contradicts our choice of \(p\) and \(q\text{.}\) So the assumption was false, and \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.
